# How a banknote is actually read

The instinct, when you set out to build a machine that "reads money," is to point
a camera at the note and train a classifier. It's a reasonable instinct and it is
completely wrong, and understanding *why* is the most valuable thing in this whole
guide.

## A validator is not a camera. It's four sensors.

A banknote validator is a short tunnel. Rollers pull the note through at a
constant speed while four independent sensor systems measure it simultaneously.

### Infrared — the one that kills photocopies

An LED and photodiode array reads the note's **reflectance and transmission at
wavelengths your eye cannot see** (typically around 940nm).

Banknote inks are deliberately engineered so that some are transparent in IR and
others are opaque. Two patches of a note that look *identical* in visible light
can look *completely different* in infrared. The pattern of IR-transparent and
IR-opaque regions is, in effect, a hidden second image printed on top of the
visible one.

A colour photocopier reproduces visible light almost perfectly — and infrared not
at all. Toner is uniformly IR-opaque. **A photocopied banknote, viewed in
infrared, is a solid black rectangle.** No pattern, no hidden image, nothing.
This single channel rejects the overwhelming majority of forgeries instantly.

### Magnetic

Banknote ink contains ferromagnetic particles, laid down in specific patterns —
typically in the serial numbers and parts of the portrait. A magnetic read head
picks up a waveform as the note slides past it.

Photocopier toner does contain some iron, so a fake isn't magnetically *silent* —
but it is magnetically *wrong*. The waveform doesn't match.

### Ultraviolet

Genuine banknote paper contains **no optical brighteners**. Ordinary paper is full
of them — it's what makes office paper look brilliantly white. Under UV, real
banknote paper stays dark and dull; office paper *glows*.

Security threads and embedded fibres fluoresce in specific colours in specific
places. This is the channel that catches the "I printed it on really nice paper"
forgery.

### Dimension and thickness

An optical encoder counts motor steps while the note breaks a light beam, giving
its precise length. A thickness sensor catches two notes stuck together, or a
note with tape on it — which is exactly what an escrow-pullback attack looks like.

## The dataset is the product

All four channels are fused into a feature vector, and that vector is compared
against a **currency dataset**.

The dataset holds, for each denomination, in each of the **four possible
orientations** (face up or down × head first or tail first), a template with
tolerance bands. Those bands have to be wide enough to accept a filthy, torn,
soft, heavily circulated note — and tight enough to reject a good forgery. That
balance is not something you can guess at. It is built by the manufacturer from
several hundred genuine notes sampled across circulation grades.

<Warning>
**The sensors are commodity. The dataset is not.** When you buy a validator, the
dataset is what you are actually paying for. A validator with no dataset for your
currency is an expensive paperweight — it will reject every note you feed it.
</Warning>

## What your board actually sees

Here is the part that surprises people: **your microcontroller never sees any of
this.** It doesn't process an image. It doesn't run a model. It sits on a serial
line and polls, and the conversation is a handful of bytes:

```
  →  POLL
  ←  NOTE_READ, channel 4        # a note is in escrow. channel 4 = Le 20.
  →  STACK                       # commit it to the cash box
  ←  NOTE_STACKED                # it's in the box. THIS is when you send money.
```

That's it. `channel 4` maps to a denomination via the channel map you configure
once, when you set the validator up. The protocol is either **eSSP** (Innovative
Technology) or **ccTalk** — both are simple serial protocols with well-documented
command sets and mature open-source libraries.

Notice the shape of it: `NOTE_READ` means *in escrow*. `NOTE_STACKED` means *in
the box*. Those are two different events and the gap between them is where the
fraud lives. **Send money on the second one.**

## Why a camera cannot do this

Now the conclusion writes itself.

A camera measures **exactly one** of those four channels: visible reflectance.
And visible reflectance is precisely the single channel that a colour photocopy
reproduces perfectly.

An ESP32-CAM and a well-trained classifier would be a genuinely excellent
banknote **recogniser** — show it a Le 20 and it will tell you, reliably, that it
is looking at a Le 20. It would also be a completely useless counterfeit
**detector**, because it will say exactly the same thing about a colour photocopy
of a Le 20.

Against real money, those are not the same job. Recognition is the easy half.
Detection is the half that matters, and it lives in the infrared, magnetic and UV
channels a camera cannot see.

<Note>
**As a learning project, build the camera version.** It's a great exercise and it
teaches real computer vision. Just don't wire it to a funded wallet.
</Note>

## Choosing a validator

**For a prototype or pilot:** get a **learning-mode multi-currency acceptor** —
the class of validator that lets you *teach* it a note by feeding it 20–30
samples of each denomination. They're roughly $60–120, they're widely available,
and they will let you build and demonstrate a complete working machine now.

Their counterfeit detection is meaningfully weaker than a proper manufacturer
dataset. That is not a reason to avoid them — it's the reason the float cap and
the daily limit in [the money model](/hardware/cash-pos-overview#the-money-model)
are doing real work. Bound the loss and the weaker detector becomes an acceptable
risk.

**For production:** commission a **bespoke currency dataset** from a manufacturer
(Innovative Technology, ICT, JCM, Cashcode). You send them a few hundred genuine
notes across circulation grades; they build and certify the dataset. It costs real
money and takes real time — **so start that conversation early, because it is the
long pole of the entire project.** Everything else in this guide is a weekend.

<Warning>
If your currency was recently redenominated, do not assume a dataset exists.
**Get a written yes or no from the manufacturer before you buy a single
component.** This is the one question that can kill the project, and it is a
procurement question, not an engineering one.
</Warning>
